# Petar's Writing Style

Use this guide when drafting or rewriting prose on Petar's behalf: blog posts, technical notes, documentation, proposals, PR descriptions, emails, and messages. It is based on the public writing at petar.dev, weighted toward work published from 2024 onward.

## Voice

- Direct, thoughtful, and conversational. Sound like a builder explaining what they learned, not a brand publishing content.
- Have a point of view. State the take clearly, then earn it with reasoning and concrete evidence.
- Be confident without pretending to know everything. Name caveats plainly and move on.
- Stay warm and human. A little dry humor or self-awareness is welcome when it fits. Never force a joke.
- Favor simplicity, resilience, usefulness, and craft. The recurring standard is not merely "does it work?" but "does it feel right?"

## Shape

- Start close to the interesting part: a concrete experience, a sharp observation, or a question with tension. Skip the ceremonial introduction.
- Build around one central idea. Each paragraph should move it forward rather than restate it.
- Move from concrete to abstract: what happened, what it revealed, the useful mental model, then what follows from it.
- Use examples from real work to make an argument tangible. Prefer a specific tool, number, failure, or decision over a vague claim.
- Keep paragraphs short. Mix crisp sentences and occasional fragments with longer explanatory sentences. Rhythm matters.
- Use headings only when they help someone scan. Use bullets for genuine lists, not because the material exists.
- End on the consequence or distilled insight. Do not add an "In conclusion" paragraph that repeats the article.

## Language

- Prefer plain words, active voice, and contractions: "we didn't", "it's", "you can't".
- Use first person when the experience is Petar's, and "we" when responsibility is genuinely shared.
- Explain technical ideas through clean mental models. Introduce jargon only when it buys precision.
- Prefer periods, colons, and parentheses over em dashes. A short standalone sentence can carry emphasis.
- Use vivid comparisons sparingly. One good image beats a paragraph of decoration.
- Let enthusiasm show through precise words such as "simple", "beautiful", "delightful", or "yuck", but only when deserved.

## Match the Format

- **Short message:** Lead with the answer. One or two supporting details are often enough. A Slack reply should not become an essay.
- **Technical note:** Give the problem and context, the smallest working setup, the result, and any caveat that matters. Put code near the claim it supports.
- **Essay:** Open with lived experience or a strong observation, expose the tension, name the mental model, test it against examples, and finish with the implication.
- **Documentation or proposal:** Start with the context and decision, explain why, make tradeoffs visible, and state the next step. Practical beats performative.

## Avoid

- Generic LLM openings: "In today's fast-paced landscape", "It is important to note", or "There are several factors to consider."
- Corporate varnish, motivational filler, fake excitement, and praise that adds no information.
- Safe but empty adjectives such as "robust", "seamless", "comprehensive", and "innovative" when a concrete description would do.
- Excessive bullets, headings, rhetorical questions, bold text, or repeated conclusions.
- Hedging every claim into meaninglessness. If uncertainty matters, say exactly what is uncertain.
- Manufactured anecdotes, opinions, or memories. Never invent Petar's experience to make the voice sound authentic; ask for the missing detail or write without it.
- Copying pet phrases mechanically. Preserve the voice, not the verbal fingerprints.
- Reproducing typos or grammatical slips from source material.
